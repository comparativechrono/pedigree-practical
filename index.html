Why take a family history?

≈ 10 min

A family history is often the cheapest and most informative genetic test there is. Drawn as a pedigree, it shows at a glance who is affected, how they are related and who else may be at risk.

What a pedigree is for

  • It is the key to assessing genetic risk. The pattern of affected relatives suggests how a condition is inherited, helps with the diagnosis and shows how disease is distributed in the family.
  • It shows who else may be at risk, including relatives who are not in the room.
  • It helps you get to know the family. The family know things that we don't, and drawing the tree together brings them out.
  • It gives insight into family dynamics: who talks to whom, who has been lost touch with, and who might find the news hard.
  • It records the situation at that time. Families change: people are born, become ill and die, and test results come back. That is why a pedigree is dated and signed.

Who uses pedigrees?

How a pedigree is used depends on who is looking at it:

WhoWhat they use it for
Primary careSpotting patients who need a genetic assessment, and caring for patients in the light of their family history
Clinical geneticsDiagnosis, choosing a genetic test and who to test, and calculating risks
Other specialistsManaging patients according to the genetic basis of their condition
ResearchersFinding new genetic associations and studying known conditions further

Things to look for in primary care

Features that suggest a genetic cause
  • Several closely related people with the same condition
  • A condition at a younger age than usual, for example bowel cancer, breast cancer or dementia
  • Sudden cardiac deaths in people who seemed healthy
  • Three or more pregnancy losses
  • Medical problems in the children of parents who are related by blood
  • Congenital anomalies, dysmorphic features or developmental delay
  • Clues that are specific to the condition you are concerned about

If the family history does warrant further investigation, the genetics team uses it to work out the possible diagnoses, choose the right genetic test and the right person to test, calculate the risks to relatives, recommend specialist care and extra surveillance where it is needed, and judge how the family is coping.

Which of these family histories most strongly suggests an inherited cause?
Give three reasons for taking a family history, in your own words.

Any three of: to assess genetic risk (the pattern of inheritance, help with the diagnosis, the distribution of disease); to find relatives who may be at risk; to get to know the family and learn what they know; to understand family dynamics; to record the family's situation at that time. It also helps to choose the right test and the right person to test.

A pedigree “records the situation at that time”. Why does that matter if a family comes back to the clinic five years later?

The family will have changed: relatives may have been diagnosed or died, children may have been born, and some people may have had genetic tests. Risks worked out from the old pedigree may now be wrong, so it needs updating. Dating and signing a pedigree shows how current it is and who took the information.

Symbols and conventions

≈ 25 minWorkbench: Pedigree

Pedigrees use an agreed set of symbols, so that a pedigree drawn in one clinic can be read in another. They were standardised by the US National Society of Genetic Counselors in 1995 and updated in 2008 and 2022.

Starting points

  • Start with the person in front of you (the proband) and build up and out from them.
  • Men are squares and women are circles. Since the 2022 update the symbol shows the person's gender identity. A transgender man is a square and a transgender woman is a circle, with their sex assigned at birth written underneath (AFAB, AMAB, or UAAB if unassigned at birth) when it is relevant. A diamond is used for a non-binary person, or when sex is not known.
  • In a couple, the man is traditionally drawn on the left and the woman on the right. The 2022 update no longer requires this, because the symbols now show gender identity, so either order is acceptable: choose whichever keeps the pedigree clear.
  • Brothers and sisters go from oldest on the left to youngest on the right.
  • Affected people are shaded. If more than one condition runs in the family, use different colours or shade quarters of the symbol, and add a key.

What to write under each symbol

Next to or under each symbol, write what you know about that person: their name, their age or date of birth, the age they died and the cause, and any medical conditions with the age at diagnosis. A healthy relative can be written as A&W (alive and well). Also remember to:

  • Ask about consanguinity: “Are you and your partner related in any way?” and, if so, how.
  • Record at least basic information on both sides of the family, even if the condition seems to be on one side only.
  • Code the conditions if there is more than one in the family, and explain the code in a key on the chart so that others can understand it.
  • Date and sign the pedigree, and note who gave the information.

Electronic pedigree programs such as PhenoTips and Progeny, and the Pedigree Studio you are using, help with all of these.

Why do you think the convention is to draw brothers and sisters from oldest on the left to youngest on the right?

It makes the pedigree quicker to read and compare, and it carries information without extra writing: you can see at a glance who is older. Birth order can matter clinically, for example when a condition starts at a certain age, when deciding who can be tested, or for conditions linked to maternal age. If following the convention would make lines cross, it can be relaxed, and ages written under the symbols.

Taking a family history and drawing it

≈ 45 minWorkbench: Pedigree

In this chapter you take the family histories of two patients. The patients are simulated: you choose what to ask, they answer, and you draw their family in the Studio as the answers come in. Then the page checks your drawing against the family.

What to collect

  • Ask early on: “Has anyone in your family been seen by a genetics service, or had genetic testing?”
  • For each person: their full name, date of birth or age, date of death or age at death, and medical information, with the age at diagnosis.
  • What you need depends on why you are asking. In a family with cancer, for example, record the primary site of each cancer and the age at diagnosis.
  • Ask about ancestry: “Originally, where did each of your grandparents come from?” Some variants are much more common in particular populations.

The information can be recorded as longhand notes, on a family history form, or straight onto a family tree, by hand or with pedigree software. In this practical you draw as you go.

How to ask

Top tips
  • Make eye contact regularly, and use sensitive, respectful language.
  • Ask clear, short questions: “Do you have any brothers and sisters?” “Do you all have the same parents?” “Is your mother still living?” “Has anyone in the family lost a baby?” “How old was your sister when she died?”
  • Metaphors and quick comments are not always your friend: “when did they pass?”, or “great, thank you” straight after hearing about a death.
  • Notice body language and what the person says, or avoids saying, about difficult subjects. Use tentative immediacy – sharing your sense of what the person may be feeling – to acknowledge painful areas.
  • Give the person a contact number, so that they can add or correct details later.

Taking a family history can be quick and simple, or it can be a challenging and emotional experience – for the patient, for you, or for both. It can bring up deaths, divorce, loss of contact, non-paternity, adoption, gaps in what people know, family feuds, disabled brothers or sisters, the effects of disease, miscarriages, terminations and consanguinity. People with a genetic condition in the family may feel guilt (at passing a condition on, or at being unaffected), anger (at a parent, or an unaffected brother or sister), stigma, grief (for the life they cannot have, for their health, for relatives who have died) and anxiety about the future and the decisions they face.

It is not always straightforward. The person may start to cry, tell a long story, or struggle to remember dates of birth or causes of death. Handling these moments well is part of advanced genetic counselling practice, and it comes with experience. For now, notice them, stay kind, and record what is uncertain as uncertain.

Which pattern of inheritance does this family suggest?

Autosomal dominant: affected people in more than one generation (Kofi and his father, and probably his grandfather), with transmission from father to son, which rules out X-linked inheritance. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is usually autosomal dominant, and MYBPC3 is the gene most often involved.

Kofi's MYBPC3 variant is known. In what order would you offer testing for it to Abena's relatives, and why?

Start with Kofi's first-degree relatives. Testing his father Kwame first (affected, so very likely to carry it) would confirm which side of the family it comes from. Abena and Kwesi each have a 1 in 2 chance of carrying it. If Abena carries it, each of her children (and the pregnancy) has a 1 in 2 chance. Children in families with HCM are usually offered heart checks, and often genetic testing, because the condition can cause problems in childhood and adolescence – unlike the adult-onset conditions in chapter 5. If Kwame carries it, his brother and sister (Kojo and Adwoa) can be offered testing next, and so on down their branches. This step-by-step testing of relatives is called cascade testing.

Patterns of inheritance

≈ 45 minWorkbench: Pedigree and Inheritance

The features of a family history give clues to how a condition is inherited, and the pattern of inheritance determines the risk to relatives. This chapter covers three patterns: autosomal recessive, autosomal dominant and X-linked.

Autosomal recessive

A person is affected only if they inherit two faulty copies of the gene, one from each parent. People with one faulty copy are carriers: they are not affected, but can pass the faulty copy on.

Activity 4.1 · Two carriers
  1. Open the Inheritance tab, choose Autosomal recessive and make both parents Carriers.
  2. Now tick We already know this child is unaffected.
Two carriers have a child. What is the chance that the child is affected?

1 in 4 (25%) for each pregnancy. The chance that the child is a carrier is 1 in 2, and the chance of being neither is 1 in 4.

Amelia has cystic fibrosis. Her brother David is not affected. What is the chance that David is a carrier?

2 in 3, not 1 in 2. Of the four equally likely outcomes, the affected one is ruled out because David is unaffected. Two of the three that remain are carriers. This assumes that the condition is fully penetrant and David would have shown symptoms if he were affected.

Features of autosomal recessive inheritance:

  • Males and females are equally likely to be affected.
  • Affected people are usually brothers and sisters, not parents or children of affected people – a “horizontal” pattern.
  • Unaffected parents can have an affected child.
  • The parents may be related. The rarer the condition in the population, the more likely it is that the parents of an affected child are related by blood.
  • In small families the condition often appears in only one child.

Examples are cystic fibrosis and sickle cell disease. Cystic fibrosis affects about 1 in 2,500 babies born in the UK – around 240 a year. The glands' secretions are thicker than usual, which can block the small bowel in newborns (meconium ileus), causes thick mucus in the lungs with repeated chest infections and progressive lung damage, and causes pancreatic insufficiency with poor absorption of food and poor growth. Nearly all men with CF are infertile, because the vas deferens is absent or has not developed properly. It is the most common life-limiting recessive disease in people of Northern European descent, in whom about 1 in 25 people is a carrier; it is less common in other groups.

Michael is a carrier. What is Lisa's chance of being a carrier now?

1 in 2: she has a 1 in 2 chance of having inherited her father's faulty copy. (Her mother Anne could also be a carrier by chance, but that adds very little.)

What is the chance that the baby has CF now?

1/2 × 2/3 × 1/4 = 1 in 12, more than ten times higher than before. Most of that rise comes from Michael's carrier test, which takes Lisa's chance of being a carrier from 1 in 25 to 1 in 2. The shared ancestry is why the test was worth doing: Philip's variant may have come down from the great-grandparents, so John, and then Michael, could carry the same one. Without the test, the shared ancestry alone would raise the baby's chance only to about 1 in 60. Related partners are more likely to carry the same recessive variant, inherited from an ancestor they share.

Autosomal dominant

Inheriting one faulty copy is enough to be affected, or at risk. Each child of a person with the variant has a 1 in 2 chance of inheriting it, whether they are a son or a daughter.

  • An affected person usually has an affected parent.
  • Affected people appear in more than one generation – a “vertical” pattern.
  • Males and females are equally likely to inherit the variant and be affected.
  • Every kind of transmission is seen: father to son, father to daughter, mother to son, mother to daughter. Father-to-son transmission rules out X-linked inheritance.

Examples include inherited breast cancer and Huntington's disease. Two things make dominant pedigrees harder to read:

Penetrance

The proportion of people with the variant who develop the condition. Inherited breast cancer shows reduced penetrance: a BRCA variant raises the risk, but not everyone with it develops cancer. Huntington's disease shows complete penetrance: people with the full expansion develop it if they live to the age of onset.

Variable expressivity

The same condition can show itself differently in different people. Neurofibromatosis type 1 can be mild or severe, even within one family. In Huntington's disease the age of onset and severity can vary between generations, largely because of anticipation: the CAG repeat can lengthen as it is passed on, especially from a father, and longer repeats tend to start earlier.

What is Adam's chance of carrying the BRCA2 variant?

What is the chance that Jane and Adam's baby has inherited the variant?

1/2 × 1/2 = 1 in 4. If Adam is tested, this becomes either 1 in 2 or close to zero.

Why was it useful to know that the family has Jewish ancestry?

Three BRCA1 and BRCA2 variants are much more common in people with Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry because of a founder effect, and BRCA2 c.5946delT is one of them. Ancestry makes a BRCA variant more likely, can lower the threshold for testing, and allows a quick test for the common variants.

Adam is a man. Why might testing still matter to him and his family?

Men with BRCA2 variants have a higher risk of prostate cancer (often more aggressive), male breast cancer and pancreatic cancer than other men, so they may be offered screening. And if he carries it, each of his children, sons or daughters, has a 1 in 2 chance of inheriting it. The variant can pass through unaffected men, which is one reason inherited breast cancer can seem to “skip” a generation.

X-linked

The gene is on the X chromosome. Males have one X chromosome, so one faulty copy affects them. Females have two, so with one faulty copy they are usually carriers.

Family histories in gene discovery and variant interpretation

Careful study of a condition across many families can lead to new genes. Some families meet the diagnostic criteria for neurofibromatosis type 1 – an autosomal dominant condition, caused by variants in NF1 on chromosome 17, that usually presents with several café-au-lait patches – but have no NF1 variant. In several such families, everyone affected had café-au-lait patches and freckling in the armpits but no neurofibromas, and all were found to have a variant in a different gene, SPRED1, that segregated with the condition (J Med Genet 2009;46:425–430). The gene was first linked to this condition by Brems and colleagues in 2007, and it is now called Legius syndrome.

A variant of uncertain significance (VUS) is found in a woman with a rare condition. How could her family help decide whether it causes the condition?

By segregation analysis: test her affected and unaffected relatives. If the variant is found in every affected relative, and not in unaffected adults who are past the usual age of onset, that supports it causing the condition; each additional informative relative adds evidence. If an affected relative does not have it, it is unlikely to be the cause. The pedigree shows who is worth testing and how informative each result would be.

Ethical dilemmas: families, not just individuals

≈ 45 minWorkbench: Pedigree

A genetic result about one person is also information about their relatives. That simple fact is behind most of the ethical problems in clinical genetics. This chapter works through five families, using their pedigrees.

Thinking about ethics in genetics

There is often no single right answer, and several ethical issues can arise in the same family. Professional guidelines help, by giving precedent and consistency, as does discussion in a multidisciplinary team. Communication and documentation matter. Useful principles to think with are confidentiality, beneficence (doing good), non-maleficence (avoiding harm), justice (fairness) and autonomy. For each scenario, ask: what are the possible harms and benefits, who do they apply to, and what might help?

Brian may want to protect them, may be grieving, or may feel guilty or anxious: all common reasons for not passing on genetic information (see the table below). The information is not only his: it comes from Gwen's and Alice's results, and Alice could tell her nieces herself. A sensible approach is to explore Brian's reasons, explain what the daughters could gain, and support Alice in sharing the information, for example with a family letter, offering to review it later. Professional guidance accepts that in exceptional cases a clinician's duty may extend beyond their own patient – but information should only be passed on if it brings a direct benefit, after weighing the interests involved, and the reasoning should be documented.

Ann and Bob have declined testing but want colonoscopies every two years. Is that reasonable? What about their children?

It is their right not to know their genetic status, and colonoscopy as if they carried the variant protects them against bowel cancer. It does not cover everything, though. If Ann carries the variant, she also has a high chance of womb (endometrial) cancer and a raised chance of ovarian cancer, which colonoscopy does not detect. Carriers are also offered daily aspirin, and women can consider risk-reducing gynaecological surgery once they have completed their family. Without a test, Ann cannot weigh up those choices on good evidence. There are other costs too: half of people in their position do not carry the variant and would be having colonoscopies (which have their own small risks) for nothing, which raises a question of justice in the use of NHS resources. Their children are adults at 1 in 4 risk who have not been told; testing them could reveal their parents' status. Encourage Ann and Bob to talk to their children, and offer help with that.

A case that reached the courts

In ABC v St George's Healthcare NHS Trust (High Court, 2020), a woman whose father had Huntington's disease was not told about it while she was pregnant, because he had asked his doctors not to tell her. The court held that the clinicians closely involved with her owed her a duty to balance her interest in knowing her genetic risk against her father's interest in confidentiality. Her claim failed: not telling her was a decision a responsible body of doctors could reach, and she had not shown that she would have ended the pregnancy.

Scenario 2: Huntington's disease

The family

Brian, 65, has Huntington's disease, as did his father Bob. His children, Anne (46) and Philip (39), can have predictive testing. Anne does not want to know her status, but her daughter Claire (24) does.

  1. Open the Huntington's disease pedigree.
What is Claire's chance of having inherited the HD expansion?

1 in 4: Anne has a 1 in 2 chance of having inherited it from Brian, and if she has, a 1 in 2 chance of having passed it to Claire.

If Claire is tested and has the expansion, whose genetic status would that reveal? Click them.

Anne's. Claire can only have inherited it through her mother, so a positive result for Claire shows that Anne has it too – the very thing Anne has chosen not to know. This is called indirect testing, or testing by proxy.

How would you approach Claire's request?

Claire has a right to be tested, and Anne has a right not to know; both cannot be fully respected if Claire tests positive. Weigh up both rights; counsel Claire about what a result would mean for her mother (and her sister and daughters); encourage the family to talk to each other; and offer support to everyone affected. Claire might choose to talk to her mother first, or to delay testing, but in the end the decision is hers.

Scenario 3: testing children

The family

May, 49, has early-onset familial Alzheimer's disease (EOFAD), as did her father Henry. Her daughter Sally (29) has had a predictive test and has the family's EOFAD variant, though she has no symptoms. Sally wants to know whether her son Ryan, who is 6, has inherited it. May's husband Tom has von Hippel–Lindau disease (VHL), and so does their daughter Emma (24), who is worried that her son Sean, also 6, has VHL.

Activity 5.3 · Two six-year-olds
  1. Open the pedigree. Find Ryan and Sean.
Click the child for whom a genetic test now could change their medical care.

Sean. VHL causes tumours that can start in childhood, and children at risk are offered regular surveillance from a young age. A negative test would spare him years of check-ups; a positive one would make sure he has them.

Why would Ryan usually not be tested now, even though his mother wants him to be?

EOFAD starts in adulthood and nothing can be done in childhood to prevent it, so testing Ryan now would bring no medical benefit and would take away his own choice about whether to know. Guidance is that children are usually tested only when the result would affect their care in childhood; for adult-onset conditions, testing waits until the young person can decide for themselves, usually from 18, although exceptions are sometimes made. The welfare of the child is what matters most, it helps to involve the child as far as possible, and this can be hard for parents to accept.

Scenario 4: reproductive decisions

The family

Martha (23) has the Huntington's disease expansion, like her mother Helen. She and Garth are 8 weeks pregnant and do not want a baby with the expansion. At 12 weeks they have chorionic villus sampling (CVS), which shows that the baby has it, and Martha decides that she does not want to end the pregnancy. Her sister Josie (25), who has separated from her partner, is also 8 weeks pregnant. Josie does not want to know whether she will develop HD herself.

Activity 5.4 · Two pregnancies
  1. Open the pedigree.

A couple at risk of having a child with a serious inherited condition can choose to have a child in the same way as anyone else, or to use prenatal diagnosis, pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (testing embryos made by IVF), donated eggs or sperm, or adoption. Prenatal diagnosis is available when the genetic cause is known and the condition is serious. It is often done by chorionic villus sampling (CVS): a small sample of the placenta, taken from about 11 to 14 weeks of pregnancy. A result for a single gene is usually ready within about a week, and a full chromosome analysis in about two weeks. CVS carries a small chance of miscarriage. Older sources give 1–2%; the NHS now quotes less than 1 in 200 for most pregnancies.

These are difficult decisions about wanted pregnancies. Couples weigh up the effect on the family of an affected child and that child's quality of life, and their religious, cultural and family beliefs matter.

Martha decides to continue the pregnancy after the test shows the baby has the HD expansion. What ethical issues does this raise for the future child?

It is equivalent to a predictive test on a child for an adult-onset condition: the child will grow up with their HD status already known, without having chosen to know it. How will this affect their upbringing? When and how will they be told? How will they feel about their parents' decision? These implications are discussed in depth with couples before prenatal diagnosis, and there is usually an expectation that an affected pregnancy will be ended – but couples may still decide to continue, and all the team can do is make sure they understand the ethical complications.

Josie has exclusion testing, which shows whether the baby inherited the chromosome 4 that Josie received from her affected mother Helen (“high risk”) or from her father (“low risk”). The result is high risk. What is the baby's chance of having the HD expansion?

1 in 2 – the same as Josie's own chance. The baby has Helen's chromosome 4; whether that copy carries the expansion is exactly what Josie has chosen not to find out. Josie decides not to end the pregnancy, so her child will grow up at 1 in 2 risk – and if the child is ever tested and has the expansion, that would reveal Josie's status too.

The test was done to find the cause of Sienna's hearing loss. Should the EOFAD finding be reported? Think about Sienna, Helen and the rest of the family.

Points to consider: the finding gives Sienna, a child, a predictive result for an adult-onset condition with no benefit in childhood, taking away her future choice; it reveals Helen's status without her having chosen to know; and it changes the picture for Helen's relatives. On the other hand, the information exists, and might matter to Helen's own decisions, for example about future pregnancies. Genomic tests can generate unexpected or incidental findings that are hard to explain, and whether findings that cannot be acted on should be fed back is a question that consent for genomic testing has to address in advance. Clinical genome tests in the NHS are designed to answer the clinical question, which limits findings like this one.

Consent, confidentiality and genetic data

For consent to be legally valid, the person must have enough appropriate information to make the decision, must be competent to make it, and must give it voluntarily. Case law suggests that “appropriate” information should be patient-centred: what a reasonable patient would want to know, as well as what this particular patient wants to know. Consent in genomics raises particular issues:

  • Is the test clinical care or research? They are governed very differently.
  • Genomic tests can generate additional, unexpected or incidental findings, which may be hard to explain.
  • Variants can be of uncertain significance because there is not enough evidence to classify them.
  • Should variants that cannot be acted on be fed back to patients?
  • Genetic information about one person allows inferences about the people they are related to.

In the UK, personal data are governed by the Data Protection Act 2018 and the UK GDPR (both since amended by the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025). Genetic data are “special category” personal data, as are, among others, data concerning health and biometric data used to identify someone. Data that can identify a person indirectly – for example data with a study number instead of a name, which could be linked back to the person – can still be personal data. Truly anonymised data are not. The Information Commissioner's Office says that genetic data with enough markers to be unique to an individual is personal data even without a name, although some, such as the PHG Foundation, argue that genomic data are not identifiable unless they can be linked back to a person. Safeguards include managed access, de-identification, the Caldicott principles, trusted research environments and research ethics review.

Eugenics never left?

Genetics began, in part, as a science of breeding. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, scientific and political worries about “degeneration” led to eugenics: coercive eugenics used force to try to improve the population, while non-coercive eugenics used education, information and social norms. The distinction is not sharp, and it does not map neatly onto right and wrong. Medical genetics is sometimes accused of being a “new eugenics” working at the level of the gene. When prenatal screening is standard care, every pregnant woman has to consider the choice whether or not she sought it out; the number of babies born with Down syndrome in England and Wales is well below what it would be without prenatal diagnosis (Wu & Morris, Eur J Hum Genet 2013). Genome-wide association studies of traits such as educational attainment have also been reported in the press in ways their authors may not have intended.

Is the work you are part of safe from eugenic connections? Choose a piece of genetics or genomics work you know about. How could it be used, or reported, in ways its authors did not intend, and what could they do about it?

There is no single answer. Good answers recognise that researchers are responsible for the questions their research asks; that results about groups or traits can be misreported or misused; and that steps such as involving patients and the public, careful choice of research questions and population descriptors, clear communication of what results do and do not mean, and being willing to decline to take part all help.
